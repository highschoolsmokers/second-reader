# Thesis — Maxwell × Bruner

**Subject**: William Maxwell, *So Long, See You Tomorrow* (1980)
**Theoretical framework**: Jerome Bruner, narrative psychology — especially *Acts of Meaning* (1990) and *Making Stories: Law, Literature, Life* (2002); the "subjunctive mode" and autobiographical self-making
**Venue**: Literary magazine
**Target length**: 8,000 words

## Core claim

Maxwell's narrator doesn't tell the Cletus Smith story in order to recover what happened; he tells it because narrative — in Bruner's sense — is the only instrument available for assembling a self that can survive having failed a friend. The novel's much-noted formal hesitations (the counterfactuals, the borrowings from the Pratt murder case, the Giacometti ekphrasis, the interleaved present-tense Lincoln, Illinois) are not aesthetic tics but the working apparatus of Brunerian self-making under conditions where memory, guilt, and the subjunctive have collapsed into one another.

## Pressure points

- **The Giacometti sculpture as narrative thesis.** The narrator reads *Palace at 4 A.M.* as a model for his own reconstructive practice; this is the essay's hinge where Maxwell's poetics meets Bruner's claim that the self is a "distributed, perspectival, transactional" construction (not a retrieved fact).
- **The counterfactuals and Bruner's subjunctive mode.** Maxwell's "what might have happened" chapters about Cletus and Lloyd Wilson are not speculative filler; they enact Bruner's claim that narrative lives in subjunctivizing reality — trafficking in possible worlds rather than actual ones.
- **The missed greeting on the school corridor.** The novel's traumatic core — the narrator failing to acknowledge Cletus at school — is where the essay pressures *both* framework and text: Bruner on guilt-as-narrative, but also the places Bruner's optimism about narrative repair doesn't fully cover what Maxwell stages.
- **The borrowed materials (newspaper accounts, the Pratt case).** Maxwell's fusion of archival research with memory implicates a Brunerian point: autobiographical narrative is never first-personal in any pure sense; it is always already a transaction with the stories the culture makes available.

## What the essay argues against

- The purely **elegiac** reading of the novel (Maxwell as lyricist of midwestern loss) that treats the formal features as mood-setting rather than epistemological.
- The purely **trauma-theory** reading (Caruth-inflected) that locates the novel's power in unrepresentability. Bruner gives us a different vocabulary: narrative *can* represent, but only subjunctively, and the subjunctive is doing real cognitive-moral work.
- A purely **Bruner-applied-to-literature** reading that flattens the text into an illustration. The essay has to show the novel pressuring the framework back — specifically, the places where Bruner's model of narrative-as-repair meets something it cannot fully absorb.

## Stakes

Beyond the Maxwell scholarship (which is small and mostly biographical), the essay makes two broader claims:

1. **For literary studies**: Bruner gives criticism a vocabulary for what formalist close reading has long noticed but struggled to theorize — the moral-epistemic work a novel's structural choices perform. This is a usable alternative to the trauma-theory and affect-theory frames that have dominated the conversation about memory-novels.
2. **For Bruner's reception**: literary critics have mostly ignored Bruner in favor of Ricoeur and Paul John Eakin. The essay argues Bruner is better-suited to certain novels — especially first-person memory novels that foreground their own narrative labor — because of his insistence that narrative is *folk psychology in action*, not a privileged aesthetic domain.

The essay should land not as "Bruner explains Maxwell" but as "Maxwell lets us see what Bruner's theory does when a writer takes its implications seriously."
